How this package is organized

The three review tracks
  • University IT (UIT) — data risk classification and vendor security review. Pages 3–4: what personal data exists, where it lives, who can see it, and how access is controlled and audited.
  • Procurement — vendor agreement and data terms. Page 5: every third-party service in the system, what data reaches it, and the contractual terms that matter.
  • UIT web governance — sign-off for anything on a Stanford site. Page 6: the Stanford Lifestyle Medicine page would link to our own site only — nothing runs on Stanford infrastructure — plus the accessibility posture and which decisions are institutional, not faculty decisions.
The four concerns raised
  • "Whatever documents the tool indexes get sent to a third party."
  • "Faculty generally can't sign the vendor's terms of service or put software on a Stanford website. Those are institutional decisions."
  • "The vendor may log and train on our content unless the contract says otherwise — IP and privacy issues."
  • "There are accessibility and web-governance requirements for anything on an official Stanford site."

Each is answered directly on page 7, with the honest current state and what a Stanford agreement would add.

The one-sentence summary

Palonur does not bulk-upload an indexed corpus to any AI vendor: indexing (embedding) runs in-house on our own servers, and only the short excerpts needed to answer one specific question cross to the answer model, under API terms that exclude training. We do not ask faculty to sign anything, and we treat hosting, contracting, and website placement as institutional decisions for Stanford to make.

Track 1 · UITData inventory & risk classification
Track 1 · UIT

What data exists, where it lives, and who can see it

Summary

The system holds two kinds of material: the research corpus (published papers and faculty-approved interpretations — not personal data) and a modest amount of personal data about the people who use it. No Social Security numbers, no medical records, no student records, and no payment card numbers are stored — payments are handled entirely by Stripe. The most sensitive category is reader questions, because a person may volunteer health context when asking. We propose classifying each element using Stanford's Low / Moderate / High risk scale, and we list our proposed classification below for UIT to confirm or correct.

Proposed risk classification (for UIT to confirm)
  • Research corpus (published studies, approved interpretations) — Low. Published material; no personal data.
  • Reader questions & answers — Moderate. Free text may include volunteered health context; stored with a session identifier, not a verified identity.
  • Subscriber contact data (email, optional phone for text check-ins) — Moderate.
  • Faculty account data (name, Stanford email, title, photo, bio) — Moderate; directory-type information, but access-controlled.
  • Access-audit records (IP address, browser user-agent, coarse city) — Moderate.

Who can see what

Summary

Every surface has its own lock. Faculty sign in through a managed identity provider. Subscribers sign in through a link emailed to them. Admin and governance dashboards need separate passwords. Outside platforms need an issued key. Nobody sees more than their own role requires, and reader questions are never shown publicly with any identifying information.

Authentication model, per surface
  • Faculty portal — Clerk (managed identity provider); role-based pillar memberships; deactivation and archiving enforced in middleware, not just the UI.
  • Consumer / subscriber area — emailed magic-link sessions (signed, HTTP-only cookies); no passwords exist to leak.
  • Admin & governance dashboards — separate password gates that fail closed: if a password is not configured, the surface denies access rather than opening up.
  • Partner / API access — individually issued, metered keys with rate tiers; revocable per partner.

Track 1 · UITData flow, retention & auditing
Track 1 · UIT

How data moves, how long it stays, and how access is audited

The data flow for one answered question

1 · Reader asks

The question arrives at Palonur's server.

→
2 · In-house search

Palonur's own embedding model (self-hosted) finds the matching approved excerpts. Nothing leaves.

→
3 · One API call

The question plus only those excerpts go to the answer model (Anthropic Claude) for that single answer.

→
4 · Cited answer

The answer returns with citations; a guard blocks any claim that lacks a matching approved source.

Retention & deletion — summary

Questions and answers are kept so faculty can see what readers actually ask (that is how coverage gaps are found) — with a session identifier, not a name. Subscribers can unsubscribe from every email, and a single STOP text opts a phone number out account-wide. When a faculty member is archived, their portal access ends immediately. Data deletion requests are honored on request; under a Stanford agreement, retention schedules would be set to Stanford's requirements.

Access auditing — current state
  • Governance dashboards — every open of the OTL/review dashboards is logged (time, IP, coarse city), including returning sessions, so the audit trail has no silent gaps.
  • Faculty actions — approvals, edits, and role changes are attributable to the signed-in faculty account.
  • Admin "view as" — when an admin previews a faculty member's portal it is read-only by construction; writes never execute as the target user.
  • Rate limits & abuse budgets — anonymous costed endpoints are rate-limited per client IP as reported through the hosting proxy; audit records capture the reported IP and browser signature.

Track 2 · ProcurementThird-party services & data terms
Track 2 · Procurement

Every third party in the system, and what reaches each one

The complete list. Nothing is omitted here; if a service is not on this list, it does not receive data from Palonur.

  • Anthropic (Claude) — the answer model. Receives, per question: the reader's question and the short text we provide as the basis for that answer. It does not browse or hold the corpus itself — it only sees what we send per question. API terms: used for inference only, not for model training.
  • Clerk — faculty identity. Receives faculty name, Stanford email, and credentials. SOC 2 Type II.
  • Resend — transactional email (magic links, newsletters). Receives recipient email addresses and message content.
  • Sendblue — optional text check-ins (SMS/iMessage). Receives phone numbers and message content for subscribers who explicitly opt in.
  • ElevenLabs — text-to-speech for the voice surface. Receives the already-generated answer text to read aloud.
  • Hosting (AWS, US West) — runs the application, the PostgreSQL database, and object storage. The corpus and all records live here.
  • External AI engines (e.g. OpenAI) — used only by the optional "how AI sees you" visibility reports, which ask public questions about the brand. No corpus text or personal data is sent.
  • Geo-IP lookup — converts an IP address to a coarse city/country for the access-audit log only.
What is contractually true today

Today these are standard commercial API agreements: the answer model's terms exclude training on API traffic, and each provider processes data only to deliver its service. What today's terms do not include: a Stanford-negotiated data processing agreement, Stanford-specific retention schedules, or a HIPAA business associate agreement (none is claimed, and none is currently needed — no medical records are processed).

What a Stanford agreement would add
  • A vendor agreement between Stanford and Palonur (not faculty) covering IP, data terms, and liability.
  • Flow-down data terms for the answer-model provider: contractual no-training, defined retention, and breach notification on Stanford's timelines.
  • Stanford-approved hosting for Stanford material — deployable into a Stanford-controlled environment, since the stack is standard Node.js + PostgreSQL with no proprietary lock-in.
  • A named data inventory appendix that UIT can re-verify at any time.

Track 3 · Web governanceSite placement & accessibility
Track 3 · Web governance

What would live on a Stanford site — and what would not

Summary

Nothing goes on a Stanford website without UIT web governance's sign-off — that is the correct order of operations and we are not asking anyone to shortcut it. What is proposed here is deliberately the lightest possible arrangement: the Stanford Lifestyle Medicine page would link to our own site only. No software runs on Stanford infrastructure at all. Faculty are never asked to accept terms of service, install anything, or place anything on a Stanford page.

The integration model
  • Link out only (no governance footprint) — the Stanford Lifestyle Medicine page links to the independently hosted site (asklifestylemedicine.com). No code, no data, no embed on Stanford infrastructure. Everything the reader interacts with runs on our own hosting.

Accessibility posture — honest current state

This section covers the one surface a Stanford page would link to: asklifestylemedicine.com. It is built with semantic HTML, keyboard-operable controls, readable contrast, and text alternatives, and is deliberately written in plain language for older readers; browser zoom is never disabled, and keyboard users get a visible focus indicator on every control. An automated WCAG 2.1 A/AA scan (axe-core) of the public ask page was run on August 14, 2026: it surfaced one contrast issue, which was fixed the same day, and the page now passes the automated ruleset with zero violations. Automated scanning covers only what machines can check — a formal third-party WCAG 2.1 AA conformance audit has not yet been performed, and we state that plainly rather than claim conformance. Before a Stanford page links out, we would complete whatever accessibility review web governance requires and remediate to Stanford's standard (WCAG 2.1 AA), producing a VPAT if procurement needs one.

Direct answersThe four concerns, one by one

Your concerns, answered directly

1 · "Whatever documents the tool indexes get sent to a third party."

Partly true, and the distinction matters. Indexing does not send documents anywhere: the embedding model that indexes the corpus runs in-house on Palonur's own servers, so building the searchable index involves no third party. What does cross to a third party is smaller and per-question: when a reader asks something, the short approved excerpts selected for that one answer are sent to the answer model (Anthropic Claude) in a single API call. So there is no bulk transfer of the corpus — but we will not claim "the corpus never leaves," because excerpt text does cross at answer time. Under a Stanford-hosted deployment, the stored corpus stays entirely inside Stanford's boundary and only those per-answer excerpts cross, under negotiated terms.

2 · "Faculty can't sign the vendor's terms of service or put software on a Stanford website."

Agreed, and the system is built on that assumption. Faculty participation requires no signature on any vendor terms: a faculty steward reviews and approves content through a portal — an editorial role, not a contracting one. The institutional decisions — the vendor agreement (Procurement), data terms and risk classification (UIT), and any placement on a Stanford site (web governance) — sit with Stanford, and this package exists to give each of those offices what they need to decide.

3 · "The vendor may log and train on our content unless the contract says otherwise."

This is the right default suspicion, so here is the current state precisely. The answer model is called under API terms that exclude training on submitted content — API traffic is inference only. Palonur itself never trains any model on Stanford material; the system is retrieval-based, so faculty-approved text is quoted and cited, not absorbed into model weights. What is not yet in place is a Stanford-negotiated contract making those commitments to Stanford directly, with defined retention and audit rights — that is exactly what the Procurement track would add, and we support flowing those terms down to the model provider.

4 · "There are accessibility and web-governance requirements for anything on an official Stanford site."

Understood, and nothing is proposed for a Stanford site without that review. The link-out model requires no Stanford-site footprint at all and can proceed while deeper options are evaluated. An internal remediation pass (August 2026) already addressed contrast, zoom, and keyboard-focus issues on the main public pages (no auditable automated result is retained yet, so we claim only the remediations themselves). For any embedded or hosted option, we would still complete web governance's accessibility review first (target: WCAG 2.1 AA) and remediate before placement — see the honest current-state note on the web governance page.
